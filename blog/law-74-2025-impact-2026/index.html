Law 74/2025 One Year On: What We've Learned

Italy's 2025 citizenship reform took effect in March 2025. Here is how the law is being applied, where the court challenges stand, and what it means if you haven't filed yet.

Published: April 11, 2026 · By Citizenship Italia Legal Team

Written by Citizenship Italia · Legal review: Avv. Marta Pignatiello, Italian lawyer Sources: Law 91/1992 · Italian Foreign Ministry Published: April 11, 2026 · Last reviewed: November 1, 2026

Short answer (as of November 2026)

The 2025 law remains in force. Italy's Constitutional Court rejected the challenges before it in April 2026, and the EU Court of Justice is reviewing it in case C-816/26. New applicants born abroad with another citizenship usually need a parent or grandparent who held exclusively Italian citizenship.

Updated November 1, 2026. This post has been corrected. An earlier version described eligibility as requiring an "Italian-born" parent or grandparent, said court cases had been centralized in Rome, and said no constitutional ruling had been issued. Those statements were inaccurate or are now out of date. For the current rules, see our Law 74/2025 guide.

The law, briefly

Law 74/2025, converting Decree-Law No. 36 of March 28, 2025, added Article 3-bis to Italy's citizenship law. If you were born abroad and hold another citizenship, you are now recognized only if a parent or grandparent holds, or held at death, exclusively Italian citizenship, or a parent lived in Italy for two continuous years after becoming a citizen and before your birth. Cases started by 11:59 PM Rome time on March 27, 2025 (a submitted application, an appointment date already communicated by the consulate or municipality, or a filed court case) follow the old rules.

Who the law has actually affected

The most obvious group is people whose claim relied only on a great-grandparent or earlier ancestor and who had not started a case by the cutoff. Their route to a new application closed.

The less obvious group is families with an Italian-born grandparent who later became a U.S. or Canadian citizen. Because the qualifying parent or grandparent must have held exclusively Italian citizenship at the applicant's birth (or at their own death), many of these claims are also affected, even though the grandparent was born in Italy. Being born in Italy is not, by itself, the test.

The two-year residence rule helps a narrower group: it applies only where a parent lived in Italy for two continuous years after becoming a citizen and before the applicant's birth.

The legal challenges: where things stand

Constitutional Court. On April 30, 2026, Italy's Constitutional Court filed Judgment 63/2026, rejecting the challenges raised by the Court of Turin and upholding Article 3-bis.

EU Court of Justice. On July 23, 2026, in Order 147/2026, the Constitutional Court asked the Court of Justice of the European Union whether the law is compatible with EU citizenship rights. The case is registered as C-816/26 and is pending; under the ordinary procedure a ruling can take around 18 months. At least one Italian court (Catanzaro, September 2026) has postponed a post-reform case until it is decided.

Our recommendation: if you qualify under current rules, do not wait. If you don't, an EU ruling could change your position, but there is no guarantee of the outcome or the timing.

Where court cases are heard

Law 74/2025 did not centralize court cases in Rome. Since June 22, 2022, applicants living abroad file with the specialized immigration and citizenship section of the court for the area where their Italian parent or ancestor was born. Separately, a January 2026 law will move administrative applications from adults abroad from consulates to a central office at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, due to be operational from January 1, 2029.

What the €600 fees mean in practice

The €600 fees come from Italy's 2025 Budget Law (Law 207/2024), effective January 1, 2025, not from Law 74/2025. Adult applicants pay €600 each at a consulate (municipalities may charge up to €600), and each claimant pays a €600 court fee in court cases. For family groups, each applicant pays separately. These are government charges, not service fees, and we include them explicitly in every quote.

Bottom line for 2026 applicants

If you have a parent or grandparent who never held any citizenship other than Italian, you are likely eligible and should move forward. If your Italian-born grandparent naturalized abroad, or your claim relies on an earlier ancestor, have your dates reviewed before you invest in documents.
