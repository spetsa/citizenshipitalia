Did Your Ancestor's Naturalization
Break the Line?

The single date that decides most claims: when your Italian ancestor became a citizen of another country, compared with when their child was born.

Last updated: November 7, 2026

Written by Citizenship Italia · Legal review: Avv. Marta Pignatiello, Italian lawyer Sources: Law 91/1992 · Italian Foreign Ministry Last reviewed: November 7, 2026

Short answer (as of November 2026)

Usually, what matters is the order of two dates. If your Italian ancestor naturalized after their child in your line was born, the child was already Italian and citizenship passed down. If they naturalized before that child was born, they generally had already lost Italian citizenship and could not pass it on. Since 2025 the same date also decides whether a grandparent counts as "exclusively Italian" for you.

  • Naturalized after the child was born: the line usually continues
  • Naturalized before the child was born: the line usually breaks
  • Never naturalized: prove it with a certificate of non-existence
  • Naturalization papers give the exact date; family memory is not enough

Courts have generally treated a voluntary naturalization as the event that ended Italian citizenship under the old law. For an Italian woman who became a U.S. citizen through marriage rather than by applying, the analysis is different; see citizenship through a female ancestor.

Examples

Naturalized after the child was born

Your great-grandfather arrived in 1905 and became a U.S. citizen in 1925. Your grandfather was born in 1915. Your grandfather was born Italian, ten years before the naturalization, so the line continues through him.

Naturalized before the child was born

Your great-grandfather became a U.S. citizen in 1912, and your grandfather was born in 1916. By 1916 your great-grandfather was no longer Italian, so citizenship did not pass through him. Another line in your family may still work.

Why the date matters twice since 2025

Under the 2025 rules, you also need a parent or grandparent who held exclusively Italian citizenship, generally judged at your birth. An Italian-born grandparent who became a U.S. citizen before you were born usually does not meet that test, even though they passed citizenship to your parent. See Law 74/2025.

How to find the naturalization date

  • Request records from the USCIS Genealogy Program, the National Archives, or the court that granted naturalization. See our naturalization records guide.
  • Check the U.S. census: 1900 to 1930 censuses asked about naturalization status and year of arrival. They give clues, not proof.
  • If no record exists, request a certificate of non-existence from USCIS.
