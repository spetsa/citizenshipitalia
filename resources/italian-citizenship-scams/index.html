Italian Citizenship Scams:
9 Warning Signs

Most providers are honest, but the 2025 law change and long consulate waits have created openings for bad actors. Here is how to protect your family.

Last updated: November 8, 2026

Written by Citizenship Italia · Reviewed by: Aldo Ponte, Head of Citizenship Procurement (law degree, University of Turin) Sources: Law 91/1992 · Italian Foreign Ministry Last reviewed: November 8, 2026

Short answer (as of November 2026)

The biggest warning signs are guarantees of approval or a date, anyone selling consulate appointments, offers to sell Italian citizenship or a passport, and pressure to pay everything up front. Consulate appointments are booked free on Prenot@mi, consulate services use esteri.it addresses, Italy has no citizenship-by-investment program, and any court case needs an Italian lawyer whose bar registration you can check.

  • Nobody can guarantee recognition, a court date or the outcome of the EU Court case
  • Consulate appointments are free on Prenot@mi; nobody can sell you a genuine slot
  • Italy does not sell citizenship, and has no citizenship-by-investment program
  • Consulate websites and services use esteri.it addresses
  • Ask for a written fee schedule and the name and bar of any lawyer

9 warning signs

  1. They guarantee the result or a date. Recognition is decided by a consulate, a comune or a court, and nobody controls their timing or their decision. A guarantee of approval, a court date or the outcome of the EU Court case is a promise nobody can keep.
  2. They say you qualify without asking about dates. Since 2025, eligibility turns on details such as when each ancestor became a U.S. citizen and whether a parent or grandparent held exclusively Italian citizenship. A provider that says "you qualify" before asking is selling, not checking.
  3. They sell consulate appointments. Appointments are booked free on Prenot@mi and are tied to the person who books them. An offer to sell you a slot, or to "skip the line", is a red flag: you have no way to know if it is genuine.
  4. They offer to sell you citizenship or a passport. Italy has no citizenship-by-investment program, and an Italian passport is issued only to Italian citizens. Investor visas give residence, not citizenship.
  5. They push you to pay everything up front. A reputable provider explains the fees in writing, separates its own fees from Italian government fees, and tells you what happens if your case turns out not to be viable.
  6. There is no named Italian lawyer. Court cases must be filed by an avvocato registered with an Italian bar. Ask for the lawyer's name and bar (Ordine degli Avvocati), and check it.
  7. They want payment by crypto, gift cards or a wire to a personal account. These are hard or impossible to reverse. Pay a business, by a traceable method, against a written agreement.
  8. The "official" site or email is not official. Italian consulates' websites and services use esteri.it addresses, such as prenotami.esteri.it and serviziconsolari.esteri.it. Be wary of look-alike sites, and of emails or calls that claim to be from a consulate and ask for payment.
  9. Their claims cannot be checked. Be skeptical of "100% success rates", huge client counts or "official partner" badges with no detail behind them. Ask for specifics and see whether they hold up.

How to check a provider

  • Ask how they will check your eligibility under the 2025 rules before you pay the main fee
  • Ask for the name of the Italian lawyer for any court case and the bar where they are registered, then check it with that bar (Ordine degli Avvocati) or the national bar council
  • Get a written agreement with a fee schedule that separates service fees from Italian government fees
  • Search the company's name together with words like "complaint" and read what former clients say
  • Pay by a traceable method, such as a credit card, and keep every receipt and email

If you think you have been scammed

  1. Stop paying, and keep every email, contract, receipt and message
  2. Call your bank or card issuer and ask about reversing the payment
  3. Report it to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, for online fraud, to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center
  4. Contact your state attorney general's consumer protection office
  5. If someone pretended to be a consulate, tell that consulate
