Naturalization Records:
How to Find Your Ancestor's U.S. Records

The complete guide to obtaining naturalization records from USCIS and NARA — and what to do when records are missing.

Last updated: November 7, 2026

Written by Citizenship Italia · Legal review: Avv. Marta Pignatiello, Italian lawyer Sources: Law 91/1992 · Italian Foreign Ministry Last reviewed: November 1, 2026

Short answer (as of November 2026)

You need your Italian ancestor's naturalization record, or proof that none exists, to show the line was not broken and, under the 2025 rules, whether a parent or grandparent held exclusively Italian citizenship. U.S. records come from USCIS, NARA or local courts; if nothing is found, request a Certificate of Non-Existence.

Key takeaways

  • Naturalization records prove the exact date your ancestor became a citizen — critical for determining if Italian citizenship passed to the next generation
  • Records are split between the naturalizing court (or its archive), NARA, and USCIS, which holds federal files from 1906 to 1956
  • If records are missing, request a Certificate of Non-Existence (CONE) as proof of non-naturalization
  • Plan for record requests to take weeks to several months, and check current fees
  • Use genealogy sites like Ancestry.com and FamilySearch to locate records, but always obtain official certified copies

Why naturalization records matter for Italian citizenship

The naturalization date is the single most important document in determining whether your Italian citizenship chain is intact. Here's why:

If your ancestor naturalized before your applicant ancestor was born, your applicant ancestor never had Italian citizenship to inherit. If your ancestor naturalized after your applicant ancestor was born, the chain is generally preserved, including, since the Court of Cassation's July 2026 ruling, where that child was a minor but held another citizenship from birth (see our Minor Issue guide). The naturalization date matters a second time under the 2025 rules: a parent or grandparent who naturalized before your birth (or before their death) generally does not meet the exclusive-citizenship test.

The exact date matters. A difference of weeks or months can determine eligibility.

Example: Giovanni is Italian. He immigrates to America. His son Salvatore is born on June 15, 1920. Giovanni naturalizes on July 1, 1920 — just two weeks after Salvatore's birth. Because Salvatore was born before Giovanni naturalized, Salvatore has Italian citizenship. However, if Giovanni had naturalized on June 1, 1920 — before Salvatore's birth — Salvatore would have no Italian citizenship claim.

To see what your ancestor's naturalization date means for your line, read did your ancestor's naturalization break the line?

Where U.S. naturalization records are held

Naturalizations in the U.S. were granted by courts, and the paper trail is usually split between the court record and the federal file:

  • Court records (declarations of intention, petitions for naturalization, certificates of naturalization): held by the court where the naturalization took place, a state or county archive, or, for federal courts, the National Archives (NARA).
  • USCIS Certificate Files (C-files), September 27, 1906 to April 1, 1956: held by USCIS and available through the USCIS Genealogy Program. Later naturalizations are generally held by USCIS in other files (A-files) or have been transferred to NARA.
  • Before September 27, 1906: there was no federal file. Records are held by the naturalizing court, a state or county archive, or NARA for federal courts.

How to request USCIS records

  • Index search: Request a search of USCIS's historical indexes through the Genealogy Program (Form G-1041). It tells you whether a file exists and gives you its file number.
  • Record request: Use the file number to request copies (Form G-1041A).
  • Fees and timing: The Genealogy Program charges a fee for each search and each record request, and processing can take several months. Check current fees and times on uscis.gov before you apply.
  • FOIA: More recent records (for example, A-files) may instead be requested through a Freedom of Information Act request.

The files typically include the declaration of intention, the petition and the certificate details, which show the naturalization date and court. (Form N-400 is the modern application for naturalization and does not exist for most historical naturalizations.)

How to request NARA and court records

  • Online search: Search catalog.archives.gov and the major genealogy databases to find the court and date.
  • Regional archives: NARA's regional facilities hold many federal court naturalization records; you can request copies or visit in person.
  • State and county courts: For naturalizations in state or county courts, contact the court clerk or the state archive.
  • Fees and timing: Fees and processing times vary by archive and court.

Certificate of Non-Existence (CONE) — when records are missing

If no naturalization record can be found, consulates usually ask for official "no record" statements, often called a Certificate of Non-Existence (CONE). In the U.S. these typically come from USCIS (after a negative Genealogy Program index search) and, where relevant, from NARA and the county court where your ancestor lived. Your consulate's checklist says exactly which statements it requires.

Why this matters:

A "no record" result supports the conclusion that your ancestor did not naturalize in the U.S. before the next person in your line was born. Under the 2025 rules, it can also help show that a parent or grandparent held exclusively Italian citizenship.

Important caveat:

A CONE is not a guarantee that your ancestor did not naturalize. It only certifies that a record was not found. If your ancestor naturalized outside the US (in a different country), or if records were lost or destroyed, no USCIS or NARA record would exist even if naturalization occurred elsewhere.

Using genealogy websites to locate records

Websites like Ancestry.com, FamilySearch.org, and JewishGen have digitized thousands of naturalization records. These are excellent for locating your ancestor and confirming basic information.

However, important note:

Consulates and courts will not accept screenshots or copies from genealogy websites as official proof. You must obtain official, certified copies from USCIS or NARA. That said, these websites are incredibly helpful for:

  • Locating your ancestor and confirming the approximate naturalization date
  • Finding alternate spellings or names used by your ancestor
  • Identifying the state or jurisdiction where naturalization likely occurred
  • Cross-referencing with other family documents

Once you've located your ancestor on a genealogy site, use the information to request official copies from USCIS (Genealogy Program), NARA or the naturalizing court.

Common challenges in locating naturalization records

Records lost or destroyed:

Some naturalization records were lost in fires, floods, or during wars. If a consulate cannot locate a record through normal channels, USCIS can issue a CONE, though consulates may be skeptical of missing records for older cases.

Name changes or misspellings:

Your ancestor may have anglicized their name or changed it at naturalization. Search for alternate spellings and variations. Genealogy websites often flag these variants.

Records scattered across jurisdictions:

Naturalization could have occurred in any state where your ancestor lived or at a federal court. If you don't know where naturalization occurred, consult genealogy websites or state records offices to narrow the location.

Late or delayed naturalization:

Some ancestors naturalized very late in life, decades after immigration. They may not have naturalized until WWII or later. Search broadly by date and location.
